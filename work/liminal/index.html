Liminal: Long-Horizon Bimanual Manipulation with FLUX.3-Action

Two robot arms, one learned policy each, and a simple planner that makes them work together

Simone Utili, Javier Gil, Oscar Devos, Stanisław Sufin

CodeProject siteFLUX.3-ActionEvent

The Black Forest Labs track asked teams to test how well FLUX.3-Action, a World Action Model, works on a new robot, both out of the box and after fine-tuning. Ours was the XLeRobot, an open-source kit with two arms.

Adapting a single model to drive both arms at once, with a 12-dimensional action output, would have needed more data and training time than a hackathon allows. We fine-tuned one six-dimensional model per arm instead, and taught each to notice when the other arm is in the way and to cooperate with it.

  • FLUX.3-Action
  • XLeRobot
  • Behavior Trees
  • LoRA Fine-Tuning

One policy per arm, one planner on top

Each arm runs its own fine-tuned copy of FLUX.3-Action and knows a handful of short skills. A behavior tree sits above them and decides which skill runs when. This lets the robot chain skills into longer tasks, such as holding a box lid open with one arm while the other picks up objects and places them inside.

  • Robot. Two SO-101 arms on a 3D-printed station, with an overhead depth camera and a camera on each wrist.
  • Skills. Short skills learned from teleoperated demonstrations. Each one is a small LoRA adapter on top of FLUX.3-Action.
  • Planner. A deterministic behavior tree that reads a task plan and decides which arm runs which skill.
  • Checks. A vision-language model confirms from the camera images that each step really happened, and a safety layer enforces limits and an emergency stop.
  • Compute. All skills were trained in parallel and served from a remote NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000.

What we learned about language models in the loop

Following the hybrid-policy experiments in the FLUX.3-Action technical report, we also tried adding a reasoning language model that steps in when a skill fails or meets a case it was not trained for.

In practice it was too slow. Large reasoning models still respond far more slowly than specialized robot policies, and cost much more to run, which makes recovering from a failure in real time difficult.
